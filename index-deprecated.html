C. The Income–GMAL Asymmetry

You observed that regressing Income Rank on all other domains plus GMAL yields a large and significant GMAL coefficient with strong R², whereas regressing GMAL on Income (and other domains) yields weak R². You ask whether this asymmetry justifies further analysis.

Why the asymmetry exists

The asymmetry is expected and does not reflect a one-directional causal relationship. It arises because the Income Rank regression has an extremely high R² driven by the other domain variables (especially Employment, which shares 22.5% weight in the IMD and is mechanically correlated with Income). GMAL's coefficient in that regression captures the residual variation in Income that the other domains leave unexplained — which could be small in absolute terms but statistically significant with N ≈ 1,587. Conversely, when GMAL is the dependent variable, the 7 domain ranks are poor predictors because GMAL measures transport accessibility — a conceptually distinct construct that is not well-captured by any linear combination of deprivation rates.

Is this sufficient justification?

The finding is consistent with your hypothesis (transport accessibility is related to income deprivation) but it is not sufficient to establish the claim, for two reasons. First, the Income-on-domains-plus-GMAL regression is endogenous by construction: Income is a component of the dependent variable in your main model. Second, a significant coefficient in an auxiliary regression does not establish the incremental predictive contribution of GMAL to the composite deprivation measure — which is your actual research question.

How to strengthen it

Replace the auxiliary Income regression with the following, more defensible evidence chain. First, report the full pairwise correlation matrix between GMAL and each of the 7 domain ranks. This establishes the pattern of association without the endogeneity issue — you can show that GMAL correlates most strongly with Income and Barriers to Housing, which is economically intuitive (transport accessibility affects job access and service access). Second, the Elastic Net analysis directly answers the research question: if GMAL's coefficient is non-zero at the cross-validated λ (confirmed above: λ = 0.007055 in the Quadratic model), it has passed the most rigorous test of incremental predictive content in the penalised regression framework. Third, the PCA loadings analysis (Table 5b) shows that GMAL loads on a dimension distinct from the dominant PC1 factor — a latent dimension not captured by any existing domain. These three pieces together are far stronger than the auxiliary Income regression.

D. GMAL Transformation Protocol

Your hand-drawn diagram correctly captures the IoD 2025 pipeline. The critical issue is that your data contains domain ranks (which have been through the full IoD processing chain) alongside a raw GMAL score that has not been processed. To make GMAL comparable to the domain variables for combination or regression, GMAL should be put through an analogous transformation. The protocol below mirrors the IoD 2025 methodology (Technical Report, Appendix E) adapted for a single new variable.

Step 0 — Shrinkage (optional, context-dependent). The IoD applies empirical Bayesian shrinkage to each indicator before combination, borrowing strength from the OAC supergroup within the LAD to stabilise estimates in small-population LSOAs (Technical Report §3.4). For GMAL, this step is likely unnecessary: GMAL is a modelled accessibility score (not a rate based on small counts), so it does not suffer from the small-numbers instability that motivates shrinkage. If you wish to be conservative, you could shrink GMAL toward the LAD mean, but this is not required for your analysis. Recommendation: skip shrinkage for GMAL; note this in your methods section with the justification that GMAL is a continuous modelled score, not a count-based rate.
Step 1 — Rank the GMAL scores across LSOAs. Rank the GMAL scores from 1 (least accessible / most transport-deprived) to N (most accessible / least transport-deprived) across the relevant set of LSOAs. Important: because your analysis is restricted to Greater Manchester (N ≈ 1,587 LSOAs), the ranking is local. If you wished to be fully consistent with the IoD methodology, you would rank GMAL across all 33,755 LSOAs in England, but since you are not constructing a national index — only testing marginal predictive power within Manchester — a local ranking is defensible. Note: GMAL is an accessibility score where higher = better access. If you want the rank to follow the IoD convention (higher rank = more deprived), you should reverse the GMAL ranking so that the least accessible LSOA gets the highest rank.
Step 2 — Scale ranks to [0, 1]. For each LSOA, compute the scaled rank R = rank / N. Under the IoD convention, R = 1/N for the least deprived and R = N/N = 1 for the most deprived. Ensure your directionality is consistent: if you reversed GMAL in Step 1 so that low accessibility = high rank, then R = 1 corresponds to the most transport-deprived LSOA.
Step 3 — Apply the exponential transformation. The IoD 2025 uses the following transformation (Appendix E, equation E.2.1): X = −23 · ln(1 − R · (1 − exp(−100 / 23))) where ln is the natural logarithm, exp is the exponential function, and the constant 23 is the scaling parameter that ensures approximately 10% cancellation. This transforms each rank into a score on [0, 100] with an exponential distribution that spreads the most-deprived tail of the distribution. After this step, the most deprived 10% of LSOAs will have transformed scores between 50 and 100.
Step 4 — Use the transformed GMAL in regressions. Replace the raw GMAL.Score column with the exponentially transformed score from Step 3. Now GMAL is on the same distributional scale as the domain ranks in your dataset, and any regression coefficient or penalised regression weight is directly comparable.

R implementation

The following R code implements Steps 1–3:

N <- nrow(Dep_clean) # Step 1: Rank GMAL (reverse so low accessibility = high rank = most deprived) gmal_rank <- rank(-Dep_clean$GMAL.Score, ties.method = "average") # Step 2: Scale to [0, 1] R_scaled <- gmal_rank / N # Step 3: Exponential transformation (IoD 2025, Appendix E) GMAL_transformed <- -23 * log(1 - R_scaled * (1 - exp(-100/23))) # Replace raw GMAL with transformed version Dep_clean$GMAL_ExpTransformed <- GMAL_transformed # Verify: most deprived 10% should have scores roughly between 50–100 quantile(GMAL_transformed, probs = c(0.90, 0.95, 1.00))

When is transformation necessary?

Transformation is essential if you wish to make claims about GMAL's contribution relative to the IoD domain weights (e.g., "GMAL would warrant an X% weight in a revised IMD"). It is less critical if your research question is purely about statistical significance of GMAL in a regression — in that case, the monotone transformation preserves rank-ordering and significance levels, so the OLS/Elastic Net results are qualitatively unchanged. However, the coefficient magnitudes and the implicit weighting in penalised regression (where the penalty is scale-sensitive even after standardisation of distribution shape) are affected. For a rigorous paper, transform GMAL and run both specifications (raw and transformed) as a robustness check.
