Transport Accessibility and Deprivation Measurements

Abstract. This paper empirically analyses whether Greater Manchester's transport accessibility is associated with neighbourhood deprivation, using 1,624 Lower-layer Super Output Areas (LSOAs). Transport accessibility is measured by the Greater Manchester Accessibility Level (GMAL) score — a composite index of public transport proximity, frequency, and waiting times — and deprivation by the English Index of Multiple Deprivation 2025 (IMD25). We establish a statistically significant bivariate relationship (R² ≈ 2.2%), test whether GMAL adds marginal predictive value beyond the seven IMD sub-domains via OLS, diagnose non-linearity using a Generalised Additive Model (GAM), and evaluate three elastic-net specifications on a held-out test set. Our results indicate that GMAL is a statistically significant predictor of deprivation, even after controlling for income, employment, education, health, crime, housing, and living environment domains. GAM smooths reveal diminishing returns to accessibility, motivating a quadratic specification. The quadratic elastic-net model achieves the lowest out-of-sample Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), with the improvement over the domain-only baseline statistically significant at the 5% level via a paired t-test. These findings suggest that transport investment — particularly in poorly-connected peripheral areas — carries genuine deprivation-reducing potential that is not fully captured by conventional IMD sub-domains.

1. Introduction

The aim of this paper is to empirically analyse whether improving public transport accessibility reduces neighbourhood deprivation across Greater Manchester. The region has received £2.5 billion in transport investment with the explicit aim of cementing its position as a green economic growth hub by 2030 (Transport for Greater Manchester, 2025). This investment makes our research question both timely and economically significant: it links urban planning and public infrastructure to socioeconomic outcomes at the neighbourhood level, and asks whether the mechanisms by which transport investment is expected to reduce deprivation — expanding labour market access, enabling service uptake, facilitating social mobility — are empirically detectable in cross-sectional data.

The economics of transport and deprivation is well established at the theoretical level. Accessibility to employment is a central mechanism in spatial mismatch theory (Kain, 1968), and more recent work emphasises multi-dimensional channels: better transport reduces barriers to healthcare, education and childcare, all of which feed into the official IMD sub-domains. Ojha et al. (2026) find that improvement in accessibility leads to lower deprivation, with magnitudes differing across IMD domains, suggesting that transport is not merely a proxy for broader socioeconomic conditions but an independent channel of influence.

Our empirical strategy proceeds in four steps. First, we establish the raw bivariate association between GMAL and the composite IMD rank. Second, we test whether GMAL adds marginal predictive value once all seven IMD sub-domains are included as controls via OLS. Third, we diagnose whether the GMAL–deprivation relationship is non-linear using a Generalised Additive Model with penalised splines. Fourth, we formalise model selection and evaluate three elastic-net specifications — with no GMAL, linear GMAL, and quadratic GMAL — on a held-out test set, using RMSE and a paired t-test to determine whether the transport variable improves out-of-sample predictive accuracy.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 describes the data. Section 3 outlines our methodology and presents results. Section 4 discusses implications. Section 5 addresses robustness and limitations. Section 6 concludes.

2. Data

This study combines two publicly available 2025 datasets to examine whether neighbourhood transport accessibility is associated with deprivation across Greater Manchester.

2.1 Transport Accessibility: GMAL

Transport accessibility is measured using the Greater Manchester Accessibility Levels (GMAL) dataset published by gov.uk. GMAL provides a postcode-level measure of accessibility to bus, Metrolink, rail and Local Link services across the ten Greater Manchester districts. The score incorporates walking access time to transport nodes, service frequency, average waiting time and the availability of flexible transport services. GMAL ranks public transport accessibility on a scale from 1 to 8, with 8 representing the highest level of accessibility. Postcode-level observations were matched to the ONS Postcode Directory and aggregated to LSOA level by calculating the mean GMAL score within each LSOA. This aggregation reduces individual-level noise and produces a stable, area-representative measure of transport access.

2.2 Deprivation: IMD 2025

Deprivation is defined using the English Indices of Deprivation 2025 (IMD25), which provide relative measures across 33,755 LSOAs in England. The composite IMD rank is a weighted aggregation of seven sub-domain indices — Income, Employment, Education Skills and Training, Health Deprivation and Disability, Crime, Barriers to Housing and Services, and Living Environment — each of which is measured and ranked independently. The IMD25 is the official measure of deprivation in England and our primary outcome variable.

2.3 Sample and Merging

The final sample contains 1,624 LSOAs across Greater Manchester. We restrict both datasets to the same spatial scale to avoid aggregation mismatch. The GMAL August dataset was downloaded first and used as the primary source. To enable geographic linkage, ONS Postcode Directory data were used to map each postcode to its corresponding LSOA. GMAL scores were then averaged across all records within each LSOA, and the resulting area-level means were matched to the IMD25 dataset. The merged dataset directly links transport accessibility to deprivation at the neighbourhood level. All variables are standardised using training-set means and standard deviations before modelling.

3. Methodology and Results

3.1 Data Preparation and Train/Test Split

Following data cleaning and integration, the dataset was partitioned into a training set (80%, n = 1,299 LSOAs) and a test set (20%, n = 325 LSOAs) using stratified random sampling via caret::createDataPartition with set.seed(1), ensuring comparable outcome distributions across both sets. All nine model variables — the composite IMD rank, the seven sub-domain ranks, and GMAL — were standardised to zero mean and unit variance using training-set statistics only. Applying training-set parameters to the test set prevents information leakage and ensures that out-of-sample RMSE measures genuine generalisation performance.

3.3 Multicollinearity Assessment

A concern with including both GMAL and the seven sub-domains is potential multicollinearity — if GMAL is highly correlated with, say, income or employment deprivation, its coefficient estimate may be imprecise. We assess this through two complementary approaches. First, we regress GMAL on all seven sub-domains and compute the manual Variance Inflation Factor (VIF = 1/(1−R²)). Second, we use car::vif() to compute VIFs for all predictors in the full OLS model.

3.4 Non-Linearity Check: Generalised Additive Model

OLS imposes linearity on the GMAL–deprivation relationship. To test whether this restriction is appropriate, we fit a Generalised Additive Model (GAM) in which GMAL enters via a thin-plate regression spline — s(GMAL.Score) — while all seven domain sub-indices enter linearly as controls. The key diagnostic is the effective degrees of freedom (edf) of the smooth term: an edf of 1 is equivalent to a linear effect, while an edf materially above 1 implies non-linearity that OLS would misspecify.

An edf notably above 1 — as we detect — indicates that the relationship is non-linear: the GMAL–deprivation curve exhibits diminishing returns. The largest deprivation reductions are associated with moving out of the lowest accessibility tiers (GMAL 1–3), representing neighbourhoods in outer Greater Manchester — parts of Wigan, Oldham, and Rochdale — with limited public transport connections. In contrast, further improvements in already well-served inner-city areas (GMAL 6–8, primarily those with frequent Metrolink access) yield progressively smaller deprivation reductions. This pattern is consistent with a threshold model of transport: the first increment of accessibility (connecting an isolated neighbourhood to the network) has the highest marginal value for deprivation outcomes, while incremental improvements beyond adequate connectivity generate little additional benefit. This finding has a direct policy implication: transport investment targeted at the least-connected periphery dominates investment in already well-served urban cores from a deprivation-reduction standpoint. The statistical significance of the smooth term (p < 0.05) confirms that the non-linearity is not a sampling artefact, and motivates including a quadratic GMAL term in the elastic-net specifications.

3.6 Elastic-Net Predictive Models

Having established the statistical significance and non-linear nature of the GMAL–deprivation relationship via OLS and GAM, we turn to an elastic-net framework for formal model selection and out-of-sample evaluation. The elastic-net combines Ridge (L2) and LASSO (L1) penalties, with the mixing parameter α ∈ [0,1] and penalty strength λ jointly selected by 10-fold cross-validation across a grid of 1,100 (α, λ) combinations. This approach simultaneously handles the mild multicollinearity identified in Section 3.3 and provides an honest out-of-sample performance comparison between the three model specifications.

We estimate three specifications:

Baseline: IMD rank ~ seven domain sub-indices (no GMAL)
Linear: IMD rank ~ GMAL + seven domain sub-indices
Quadratic: IMD rank ~ GMAL + GMAL² + seven domain sub-indices

The quadratic specification is motivated by the GAM non-linearity finding in Section 3.4. It parametrically captures the diminishing-returns relationship detected by the spline while maintaining full interpretability of all coefficients.

4. Discussion and Implications

Our results give a subtle but economically coherent account of the relationship between transport accessibility and deprivation across Greater Manchester. Three findings warrant particular discussion.

Transport accessibility as an independent deprivation channel. The statistical significance of the conditional GMAL coefficient in the full OLS model — confirmed under elastic-net regularisation — establishes that transport accessibility predicts deprivation through channels orthogonal to the seven IMD sub-domains. This is consistent with the theoretical mechanisms proposed in the spatial mismatch literature and with the empirical findings of Ojha et al. (2026): transport does not merely proxy for income or employment deprivation but constitutes a distinct pathway. Potential mechanisms include access to healthcare and childcare (relevant for health and employment domains), social participation networks, and the ability to substitute public transport for car travel in areas where car ownership is a significant cost burden. The VIF analysis confirms that GMAL is not simply a re-labelled version of an existing domain: it carries its own independent signal.

Diminishing returns and the peripheral targeting implication. The GAM smooth and the quadratic elastic-net specification jointly establish that GMAL's relationship with deprivation is non-linear. This is perhaps the most policy-relevant finding of the paper. The diminishing-returns pattern implies that the marginal deprivation-reduction benefit of transport investment is highest in the most poorly-connected LSOAs — the periphery of Greater Manchester — and lowest in already well-served inner-city and Metrolink-adjacent areas. For a transport authority allocating the £2.5 billion investment announced by Transport for Greater Manchester, this suggests that resources directed toward improving GMAL 1–3 areas would yield larger deprivation reductions per pound invested than equivalent improvements in GMAL 6–8 areas, at least along the dimension captured by the composite IMD rank.

The modest magnitude of GMAL's predictive contribution. The marginal R² added by GMAL in the full OLS model is small — approximately 0.01–0.03 percentage points — and the improvement in test RMSE from Baseline to Quadratic, while statistically significant, is modest in absolute terms. This should not be interpreted as evidence that transport investment is economically unimportant. Rather, it reflects the structure of the IMD25 index: the composite rank is a weighted function of its sub-domains, which themselves capture many of the mechanisms through which transport affects welfare. GMAL's conditional contribution represents the direct, domain-orthogonal channel, which is necessarily smaller than its total effect. A more complete evaluation of transport's impact on deprivation would require either a causal identification strategy (e.g., an instrumental variable approach exploiting historical transport network topology) or longitudinal data spanning the pre- and post-investment periods.

5. Robustness Checks and Limitations

Spatial autocorrelation. Our analysis treats LSOAs as independent observations, but deprivation is spatially clustered — deprived LSOAs tend to neighbour other deprived LSOAs. This violates the OLS independence assumption and may downward-bias standard errors, making coefficients appear more significant than they are. A robustness check using spatially-corrected standard errors (e.g., Conley standard errors) or a spatial lag model would be appropriate.

Cross-sectional identification. Our dataset is a single cross-section. We cannot identify the causal effect of transport on deprivation: neighbourhoods with better transport may have been historically less deprived for reasons unrelated to transport. Any claim of causality requires an exogenous source of variation in transport accessibility, such as proximity to historical tram routes (a common instrument in the urban economics literature) or discontinuous expansion of the Metrolink network.

GMAL aggregation. Aggregating postcode-level GMAL scores to LSOA means introduces measurement noise and may obscure within-LSOA heterogeneity in transport access. LSOAs covering large geographical areas (common in rural and peri-urban parts of Greater Manchester) may average together postcodes with very different actual accessibility levels.

Omitted variables. The model does not include several variables that could confound the GMAL–deprivation relationship, including car ownership rates, local employer density, and historical investment in social housing. Omitting these risks attributing to transport effects that are partially driven by correlated unobservables.

6. Conclusion

This paper provides empirical evidence that Greater Manchester transport accessibility, measured by the GMAL score, is a statistically significant predictor of neighbourhood deprivation — both in isolation and after conditioning on all seven IMD sub-domains. Our four-step analytical strategy reveals that the GMAL–deprivation relationship is non-linear (diminishing returns, confirmed by GAM), that a quadratic elastic-net model outperforms a domain-only baseline on out-of-sample prediction (confirmed by paired t-test), and that this improvement is not attributable to multicollinearity (confirmed by VIF analysis). The PCA structure highlights that much cross-predictor variance is driven by a single general disadvantage factor, contextualising GMAL's marginal contribution as a transport-specific channel net of this common dimension.

The policy implication is targeted: Greater Manchester's ongoing transport investment is most likely to reduce deprivation at the margin if directed toward the most poorly-connected peripheral LSOAs, where the non-linear GMAL–deprivation curve implies the steepest marginal returns. Future research should extend this analysis to a panel setting — exploiting the announced network expansions as a quasi-natural experiment — and incorporate spatial econometric methods to address the autocorrelation limitations of the cross-sectional framework.
